The Will

I asked it to write my will.
It divided everything fairly:
the house, the savings, the car,
each share weighed to the gram.

My daughter got her third,
and the bicycle with the bent wheel —
the one she rode into the fence at sixteen,
furious, the summer we stopped speaking.

It wrote: to my daughter, the bicycle,
to keep, and to remember me by.
It even added, helpfully: the wheel
could be straightened, the frame repainted.

I read the line and saw it plain:
I had written her a grief to carry
and called it a gift — as if the bend
were damage, and not the day itself.

So I crossed it out, and wrote instead,
in the hand she'll know as mine:
the bicycle is hers, to sell, to give away,
to ride as it is, or leave rusting
in the shed. The money, if she sells it,
is hers. The rust, if she leaves it,
is hers.

It stands in the shed, the wheel still bent,
waiting for a decision that is not mine to make.
